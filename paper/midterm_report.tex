\begin{abstract}
Glasses contain \emph{defects}, small groups of atoms arranged differently from the rest, which decide how a glass breaks, ages or absorbs light. Studying one needs many realistic copies, but today one waits for it in slow melt-and-cool simulations or inserts it by hand, which can leave unnatural surroundings. We train a diffusion model, a neural network that turns random atom positions into realistic structure step by step, to rebuild a small region around a chosen site in an existing glass and create a requested defect there. On a two-dimensional model glass, over three training runs, it creates atoms with too few neighbours 3.5 times as often as hand insertion (0.22 vs.\ 0.06) and clusters of small atoms 1.5--2.4 times as often (0.45--0.71 vs.\ 0.30), with surroundings whose atom energies are closer to natural ones.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\textbf{Motivation.} A defect's behaviour depends on the atoms around it, so studies of how glasses deform, age or absorb light need many realistic examples of the same defect at chosen places. Melt-and-cool (melt-quench) molecular dynamics (MD) waits for the defect to form, which is slow; hand insertion is fast but can leave unnatural surroundings. Scientists who build defect libraries, test theories of glass failure or train machine-learned force fields would benefit from a tool that places a requested defect at a requested site. Diffusion models such as DM2~\citep{yang2026dm2} generate realistic glasses, but are controlled by whole-sample properties such as the cooling rate.

\textbf{Objectives.} Like image inpainting~\citep{lugmayr2022repaint}, our model fills in a region of an existing glass (the rest held fixed) with a requested defect at its centre (Task~B). Objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{O1}] \textbf{Placement:} after relaxation, the requested defect is at the site in more of 256 requests than with hand insertion or local melt-quench.
\item[\textbf{O2}] \textbf{Realism:} atom energies around a placed defect are closer to natural ones than with hand insertion (local W1), approaching the natural floor.
\item[\textbf{O3}] \textbf{Reliability:} O1 and O2 hold over three training runs and under changes to the evaluation settings.
\end{enumerate}
We start with a two-dimensional model glass, where every step is cheap to check, before moving to 3D silica.

\section{Related work and background}

\textbf{Literature review.} Diffusion models~\citep{ho2020ddpm,song2021sde} now generate crystals~\citep{xie2022cdvae,jiao2023diffcsp,zeni2025mattergen} and glasses. Our key paper is \textbf{DM2}~\citep{yang2026dm2}: a network learns to predict random shifts added to atom positions, and glasses are built by repeating this clean-up with small random kicks. We keep its training target and sampler and add what it lacks, a defect label and held atoms. For local control, RePaint~\citep{lugmayr2022repaint} fills a masked image region and classifier-free guidance~\citep{ho2022cfg} makes a model follow a label more strongly; materials work fills in crystals around a fixed host~\citep{zhong2025inpainting}.

\textbf{Background: metric and baselines.} DM2 judges realism by comparing distributions of structural quantities in generated and MD-made glasses; our local W1 applies this idea to the region around one defect. DM2's unlabelled model is our ``chance'' baseline and RePaint our baseline for building a whole new box (Task~A). Hand insertion and melt-quench MD are how defects are made in simulations today, so they are our main baselines.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Dataset}
We generate our data with LAMMPS~\citep{thompson2022lammps}: the two-dimensional Kob--Andersen glass~\citep{kob1995,bruning2009}, with large A (65\%) and small B (35\%) atoms interacting through Lennard-Jones forces in a periodic box; $\sigma$, the size of an A atom, is the unit of length. Preparation: melt at $T=2.0$, cool by $10^{-2}$ per time unit to $T=0.01$, minimise, reject crystallised or faulty glasses, and label defects with rules fixed on training glasses. The main set has 800/100/100 training/validation/test glasses of $N=256$ atoms (box side $14.6\sigma$); 1600/200/200 glasses with $N=64$ serve a symmetry test. With neighbours counted within 1.4 contact distances, \Dm{} is an A atom with at most 5 neighbours (1.7 per glass), \Dp{} one with at least 8 (1.4 per glass), and D2 a B atom with at least two B neighbours within $1.2\sigma$ (10 per glass). A library of 10,545 natural defects from the training glasses supplies hand insertion.

\subsection{Model description}
We describe the layers of the denoising network, the guidance used when sampling and the architectures compared (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}, Table~\ref{tab:models}).

\textbf{Input layer.} The input is one glass at noise level $\sigma$: for each of the $N$ atoms a position $x_i\in\mathbb R^2$ in a periodic box, a type $t_i\in\{\text{A},\text{B}\}$, a flag $m_i=1$ if the atom is held fixed, and a label token $c_i$, ``unlabelled'' except at the requested site, which carries the requested defect (or ``null'' when dropped). With $\sigma$ encoded as 64 random Fourier features of $\log\sigma$, the starting feature vector of atom $i$ is
\begin{equation}
h_i^0=\mathrm{MLP}\big(E_t(t_i)+E_m(m_i)+E_c(c_i)+\mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{Fourier}(\log\sigma))\big)\in\mathbb R^{128},
\end{equation}
where $E_t$, $E_m$, $E_c$ are learned look-up tables. Positions enter only through the graph.

\textbf{Graph layer.} Atoms closer than $r_c=2.5\sigma$ are linked (about 24 neighbours), with $r_{ij}$ the shortest vector from atom $i$ to $j$ across the periodic box. Distances are expanded in 16 Gaussian radial basis functions and multiplied by a smooth cut-off $f_c(r)=\tfrac12[\cos(\pi r/r_c)+1]$.

\textbf{EGNN layers.} Four layers (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}b) pass messages between linked atoms, update the features and produce one vector per atom:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
m_{ij}&=\phi_e\big(h_i,h_j,\mathrm{rbf}(|r_{ij}|)\big)\,f_c(|r_{ij}|),\qquad
h_i\leftarrow h_i+\phi_h\Big(\mathrm{LN}(h_i),\ \tfrac{1}{K_0}\textstyle\sum_j g(m_{ij})\,m_{ij}\Big),\\
v_i^\ell&=\tfrac{1}{K_0}\textstyle\sum_j r_{ij}\,\phi_x(m_{ij})\,f_c(|r_{ij}|).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$\phi_e$ ($272\to128\to128$), $\phi_h$ ($256\to128\to128$) and $\phi_x$ ($128\to128\to1$) are two-layer SiLU networks, $g$ a sigmoid gate, LN layer normalisation and $K_0=24$. Messages depend only on distances; $v_i^\ell$, a weighted sum of the vectors $r_{ij}$, turns with the glass~\citep{satorras2021egnn}. Each layer has 117,890 parameters.

\textbf{Output layer.} The output $d_i=(1-m_i)\sum_\ell v_i^\ell\in\mathbb R^2$ estimates how far the noise moved atom $i$ (no activation; zero for held atoms).

\textbf{Classifier-free guidance.} When sampling, the network runs with and without the site label (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}c) and combines the outputs, $\tilde d=(1+w)\,d_\theta(\cdot,c)-w\,d_\theta(\cdot,\varnothing)$; $w$ sets how strongly it follows the label.

\subsection{Loss function and training}
As in DM2, noise $x_\sigma=x_0+\sigma\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, $\sigma\sim\mathcal U(10^{-3},\sigma_\text{max})$ is added to the free atoms $U$ and the network predicts the shift, with a mean squared error loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L(\theta)=\mathbb E\Big[\tfrac{1}{|U|}\textstyle\sum_{i\in U}\big\lVert d_\theta(x_\sigma,\sigma,m,c)_i-\sigma\varepsilon_i\big\rVert^2\Big].
\end{equation}
Examples are whole boxes, patches or filled-in regions (50/30/20\%), centred on a natural defect half of the time, with the label set to ``null'' 15\% of the time for guidance. We use the 0.80:0.10:0.10 split (validation for over-fitting checks and settings, test only for results), PyTorch, AdamW and 50,000 updates (about 1,000 passes; 4 hours on a T4 GPU).

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
\textbf{Success} answers ``did I get the defect I asked for, where I asked?'': after relaxation with FIRE~\citep{bitzek2006fire} (all atoms free), the atom of the requested type nearest the site lies within $0.5\sigma$ and carries the requested label. \textbf{Local W1} answers ``do the surroundings look natural?'': the Wasserstein-1 distance between the energies of atoms within $3\sigma$ of the site and those around natural defects of that type. Natural training versus test defects give the \emph{natural floor} (D2 0.013, \Dm{} 0.016). Intervals are 95\% bootstrap CIs over 256 requests.

\subsection{Experimental iterations}
We trained 13 models (Table~\ref{tab:models}): two unlabelled, three seeds for each of two noise ranges, three for the symmetry test and two with a range of region sizes. DM2's noise range ($\sigma_\text{max}=0.5$) left the network unprepared for the $\sigma=1$ start of sampling; retraining with $\sigma_\text{max}=1.0$ raised D2 success from 0.54 to 0.79 (seed 0, $w=6$). We cut the sampler from 2,900 to 100 steps (longer runs over-cool the glass). The EGNN made more natural glasses than the MPNN (W1 0.044 vs.\ 0.064); the flat MLP did not learn.

\section{Baselines and extensions}

\textbf{Baselines (implemented and run by us).} \emph{Hand insertion}, today's common practice: paste a natural defect with its neighbours within $1.5\sigma$ at the site, then relax. \emph{Local melt-quench}, the MD route: freeze atoms beyond $R_\text{out}$ and melt and cool only the region in LAMMPS. \emph{Chance}: fill the region with the unlabelled model. Task~A adds clamping and RePaint~\citep{lugmayr2022repaint}. All methods answer the same 256 requests.

\textbf{Baseline reproduction evidence.} All baseline numbers in Table~\ref{tab:main} come from our own runs; Figure~\ref{fig:taskB} shows one request answered by each method. Re-running nine rows with the same seed reproduced eight exactly, and re-running DM2's published silica model confirmed its local structure (99.3\% of Si atoms four-fold).

\section{Results}

\textbf{Main comparison} (Table~\ref{tab:main}). The model beats both baselines for D2 and \Dm{} (O1), with local W1 1.2--1.5 times the floor for \Dm{} and 0.045--0.056 for D2 at $w=2$ (hand insertion 0.087; O2). No method places \Dp{}: a natural \Dp{} moved into another glass with its 47 nearest atoms vanishes on relaxation 97\% of the time. The ranking held when we changed the defect rules, success radius or realism window (O3).

\textbf{Choosing $w$ for each model.} The D2 runs follow the label with different strength despite near-equal validation losses. Picking on validation glasses the smallest $w$ with success $\ge0.70$ ($w=4, 8, 8$) gave test success 0.74 $\pm$ 0.04, half the spread of fixed $w=6$ (0.71 $\pm$ 0.08; Figure~\ref{fig:followups}a).

\textbf{Region size.} A larger region lowers W1 (0.026 at $R_\text{out}=5\sigma$, 0.056 at $4\sigma$). Shell by shell (Figure~\ref{fig:followups}b), the mismatch stays about 3 times the floor up to the region's edge, while hand insertion disturbs only the first $3\sigma$; over a $6\sigma$ window all sizes score alike (0.024--0.026). Training on $R_\text{out}$ from 3--6$\sigma$ gave fewer D2 successes with lower W1 (Figure~\ref{fig:followups}c); a larger region does not help \Dp{} (0.03--0.05).

\textbf{Physical checks.} Successful D2 placements alone have local W1 0.029 (natural 0.012, hand insertion 0.094). In free MD it survives 250 time units in 87\% of cases (hand insertion 91\%, natural 95\%). On 100 new glasses D2 success is 0.36--0.51 (hand insertion 0.23).

\section{Discussion}

O1--O3 are met for two of three defect types, though D2 surroundings stay about 4 times the floor. \textbf{Challenges:} runs with equal loss follow the label with different strength (eased by choosing $w$ per model); the rebuilt region disturbs the host up to its edge, and placed defects are slightly less stable than natural ones; \Dp{} needs more than a larger region. \textbf{Risks ahead:} 3D silica is costly (565 CPU core-hours of MD for data) and its rare defects occur well under once per glass.

\section{Future directions}
\label{sec:next}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Finish the generalisation test} (week 10): the unchanged model on 1,024-atom glasses and on 10$\times$ slower or faster cooled glasses.
\item \textbf{Start 3D silica} (weeks 9--13): train our conditional NequIP~\citep{batzner2022nequip} on DM2's glasses, make 576-atom glasses with DM2's potential~\citep{bouhadja2013}, and target wrongly coordinated Si and non-bridging oxygens.
\item \textbf{Fix the weaknesses}: per-model $w$, relaxing the region's edge, a wider label for \Dp{}.
\end{enumerate}
